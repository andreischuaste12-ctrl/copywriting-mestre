# Complementary References

These sources extend the skill across persuasion, reader-centered language, accessibility, and responsible claims. They inform judgment; no formula replaces audience research or evidence about the offer.

## Strategy and persuasion

- **Eugene M. Schwartz, _Breakthrough Advertising_ (1966).** A framework for thinking about audience awareness, market sophistication, existing desire, and promise strength. Match the argument to what the reader already knows.
- **David Ogilvy, _Ogilvy on Advertising_ (1983).** Classic guidance on research, clear benefits, headlines, and brand consistency. Understand the product before writing; make the benefit clear without relying on adjectives.
- **Robert B. Cialdini, _Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion: New and Expanded_ (2021).** Social-influence principles including reciprocity, consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, and unity. Use them to clarify decisions, never to fabricate popularity, credentials, or urgency.
- **Robert W. Bly, _The Copywriter’s Handbook_, 4th ed. (2020).** Practical reference for structure, headlines, benefits, and editing. Treat techniques as starting points and remove what does not serve the audience, offer, or channel.
- **Joanna Wiebe, “Amazon Review Mining.”** https://copyhackers.com/2014/10/amazon-review-mining/ — practical method for extracting themes and customer language from reviews. Reviews are not automatically representative, and the method alone does not prove conversion lift. Link supplied by the audit and not opened live in this review.
- **Iwamoto, “The Origin of AIDA.”** https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jmhr/3/2/3_150/_article/-char/en — alternate article version supplied by the audit for discussing historical attribution of AIDA. Not opened live in this review; see sources.md for context.
- **Ray Edwards, PASTOR explanation.** https://www.rayedwards.com/go/031?post_page=2 — alternate primary source supplied by the audit to check the six-letter framework. Not opened live here; it does not establish universal effectiveness.

## Language, content, and experience

- **GOV.UK, Content design.** https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design — center content on what people need to do and understand. Start with the reader's need and remove material that does not help.
- **GOV.UK, Style guide.** https://www.gov.uk/guidance/style-guide — editorial conventions and plain language. Prefer familiar terms, consistency, and clear instructions.
- **PlainLanguage.gov, Federal Plain Language Guidelines.** https://www.plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/ — organize information so people can find, understand, and use what they need.
- **Nielsen Norman Group, 10 Usability Heuristics.** https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/ — interface text should use familiar language, provide feedback, and help prevent or recover from errors.
- **W3C, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2.** https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/ — accessibility standard for web content. Validate relevant technical requirements as well as copy.

## Behavior and action

- **BJ Fogg, Fogg Behavior Model.** https://behaviormodel.org/ — relates motivation, ability, and prompts to behavior. Make an action feasible and the next step visible; do not use pressure to compensate for an unclear offer.

## Truth and evidence

- **U.S. Federal Trade Commission, Advertising and Marketing.** https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing — U.S. resources on substantiation and non-misleading advertising. Verify requirements for the market where the campaign will run.

Legal and regulatory sources reflect specific jurisdictions. Before publication, confirm current requirements for the country, sector, channel, and claim type. Apply usability and accessibility references according to the product and local requirements.
