# Research, Evidence, and Positioning

Use this reference when a copy decision depends on audience research, differentiation, or interpreting a promise. Turn source material into traceable editorial decisions; never treat a hypothesis as a fact.

## 1. Start with the decision

State what the research must inform: which objection to address, which audience to prioritize, what information is missing, or whether a claim can be used. Ask a question that can be answered with evidence. Do not use copy to substitute for a product, pricing, distribution, or policy decision.

## 2. Separate evidence types

| Type | What it means | What it supports |
|---|---|---|
| Operational fact | Product feature, price, term, or figure confirmed by an accountable source | Reproduction within the source's scope and validity |
| Observed behavior | Action recorded in analytics, support, sales, or observation | What happened in the measured context; not why |
| Report | What a person said, wrote, or reviewed | That person's experience in context; not the whole market |
| Interpretation | A synthesis connecting evidence | A reasoned reading with supporting items and alternatives |
| Hypothesis | An unconfirmed explanation or prediction | A question or test, not a publishable fact |

No source is universally best. Analytics can count instrumented events; interviews illuminate context and language; product records confirm specifications; controlled experiments may estimate causal effects under their assumptions. Choose a source that matches the claim. Record source, date, context, method, excerpt or metric, sample size when known, limits, and owner. Remove personal identifiers before sharing quotes.

## 3. Read sources within their limits

- Interviews illuminate context and language; they do not estimate market prevalence.
- Open surveys provide themes and vocabulary; volunteers may differ from nonrespondents.
- Closed surveys count responses within the sample and answer choices. Record recruitment, question wording, nonresponse, and field dates. Do not call a sample representative without support.
- Public reviews can reveal themes but overrepresent people motivated to post. Do not use a review as an ad testimonial without permission.
- Support and sales logs reveal recurring questions and friction; volume may reflect a channel, policy, or operational defect.
- Search queries show terms, not necessarily intent, ability to buy, or cause.
- Analytics depend on implementation. Check missing or duplicated events, consent, filters, tracking changes, and traffic mix.
- A/B tests compare versions in the tested population and conditions. Check randomization, SRM, exposure, time window, and guardrails. Results do not automatically transfer.

When sources disagree, record the difference. Investigate population, period, method, and definitions rather than selecting the source that supports a preferred message.

## 4. Collect VoC without distorting it

Ask about past behavior and concrete situations: “Tell me about the last time you tried to solve this. What happened first?” “What alternatives did you consider?” “What nearly made you stop?” “What information was missing?” Avoid leading questions, hypothetical purchase intent as proof, and fishing for compliments. Keep the exact quote separate from interpretation.

When grouping interviews, reviews, or contacts:

1. Preserve original wording and minimum context.
2. Tag themes such as situation, task, desired outcome, frustration, alternative, criterion, doubt, cost, or product language.
3. Track evidence type and source; one person may contribute to several themes.
4. Count only when the corpus permits it. Keep frequency separate from intensity, impact, and reported urgency.
5. Look for counterexamples and themes introduced by the question itself.
6. Select representative excerpts and retain traceability to an authorized source.

A vivid quote can inspire an opening but does not prove the experience is common. Quote accurately and with permission.

## 5. Compare alternatives fairly

Map what the audience could do instead: a direct competitor, workaround, manual process, status quo, or delay. Compare equivalent criteria and do not describe competitors by assumption. A differentiator becomes an argument only when it matters to the audience and evidence supports the contrast. If no defensible difference exists, explain fit instead of inventing a USP.

## 6. Make a compact decision brief

Before writing, summarize audience and context, objective, awareness and intent, task or desired progress, current alternative, observed barrier, mechanism and proof, terms and constraints, voice and format, and unresolved gaps. Treat awareness and intent as hypotheses until evidence supports them. If several barriers are plausible, prioritize by evidence, decision impact, and cost to address; do not answer everything in one piece.

## 7. Turn evidence into copy

For each argument ask:

1. What does the source actually show or report?
2. Why does it matter to this audience?
3. What conclusion does the evidence permit, and what does it not permit?
4. What clear wording preserves conditions and context?
5. What next step fits the decision stage?

For example, “12 participants mentioned installation difficulty” supports that the theme appeared among those 12 participants. It does not support “most customers struggle” without compatible denominator, sampling, and context. If guided installation is a real feature, copy may explain it; do not claim it eliminates every problem.

Use claims-matrix.md for multi-claim campaigns, regulated categories, studies, comparisons, or numbers. After publication or testing, record version, exposed audience, date, channel, hypothesis, primary metric, guardrails, instrumentation quality, and external factors. Keep observations separate from inferences and preserve negative or inconclusive results.
