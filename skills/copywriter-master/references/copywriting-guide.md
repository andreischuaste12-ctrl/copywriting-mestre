# Copywriting Strategy Guide

Use this guide to choose an argument and structure for the audience, offer, and channel. No framework guarantees performance. Start with the decision the reader needs to make, not with an acronym.

## 1. Audience, awareness, and market sophistication

Awareness is a working hypothesis, not a fixed personality label. A person may be highly aware in one category and unaware in another.

| Working level | Likely starting point |
|---|---|
| Unaware | Recognizable situation, useful insight, or story that reveals a relevant problem |
| Problem-aware | Name the problem and its real consequences |
| Solution-aware | Explain the solution category and a meaningful mechanism or difference |
| Product-aware | Provide evidence, answer objections, state terms |
| Most-aware | Clarify offer, price, deadline if real, and CTA |

Market sophistication describes how familiar the audience is with repeated category promises. Repetition may require clearer differentiation, mechanism, and proof; it does not justify making a larger unsupported promise. Identify the current alternatives, including doing nothing. Use Jobs to Be Done as a way to understand a desired progress in context, not as proof of universal motivation.

## 2. Choose a structure only when it helps

| Structure | Sequence | Useful for | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| AIDA | Attention → Interest → Desire → Action | Simple persuasive flow | Traditional attribution to E. St. Elmo Lewis is debated; stages are not mandatory |
| PAS | Problem → Agitation → Solution | Relevant, recognized problem | Do not intensify beyond evidence or manufacture fear; origin is uncertain |
| PASA | PAS with explicit action | Short response copy | A usage variant, not a secure historical attribution |
| BAB | Before → After → Bridge | Concrete contrast | Do not imply the outcome is guaranteed |
| FAB | Feature → Advantage → Benefit | Translate a specification into audience value | Benefit must matter to the audience |
| Four Ps of copy | Promise → Picture → Proof → Push | Present and support a promise | Not the marketing-mix Four Ps; variants exist |
| Reason-why | Claim → verifiable reason to believe | Skeptical audiences or complex offers | Proprietary language is not proof |
| Demonstration | Show use → connect to benefit → CTA | Visible or observable product benefit | Demonstration must reflect real use |
| PASTOR | Person → Amplify → Story → Transformation → Offer → Response | Long-form copy | Ray Edwards' framework has expanded variants; check sources before attributing details |
| QUEST | Qualify → Understand → Educate → Stimulate → Transition | Educate before presenting an offer | Education still needs proof and a clear next step |
| Star–Story–Solution | Recognizable person → relevant story → solution | Context before a solution | Story does not replace evidence |
| StoryBrand / SB7 | Customer, problem, guide, plan, CTA, stakes, success | Brand messaging | Not a universal sales formula |
| Long-form sales letter | Promise → context → argument → proof → objections → offer/terms → CTA | Complex decisions | Length should fit complexity and intent, not a rule that longer copy converts more |

AIDA's origin is debated; PAS does not have a securely established single origin. Label editorial adaptations as such. See sources.md before repeating historical claims.

## 3. Adapt the message to the decision

| Context | Useful message order |
|---|---|
| Cold, unaware audience | Recognizable scene or insight → new perspective → benefit → low-friction next step |
| Recognized problem | Specific problem → real consequence → solution → proof |
| Comparing solutions | Decision criteria → relevant difference or mechanism → fair comparison → evidence |
| Knows the product, has questions | Benefit → demonstration or proof → objections → terms and risks → CTA |
| High purchase intent | Offer → price and terms → fulfillment or guarantee → CTA |
| B2B or high-risk purchase | Context → verifiable impact → implementation → evidence → requirements and risks → next step |
| Quick, low-involvement purchase | Recognizable benefit → decisive price or term → CTA |

### Common formats

- **Product page:** what it is and who it serves; main benefit; decision-relevant features; demonstration and proof; terms; CTA.
- **Consideration landing page:** headline and value proposition; audience and problem; mechanism; evidence; objections and requirements; offer, terms, CTA.
- **Ad:** hook; benefit or tension; proof or context when needed; CTA aligned with the destination.
- **Email:** aligned subject and preheader; context or main benefit; scannable body with one central argument; explicit CTA; essential terms.
- **Interface copy:** name the action; explain consequences before consequential decisions; show total price and terms; state what happens next; do not disguise cost or consent.

## 4. Execution principles

Use one central message per section, informative headings, concrete verbs, and mobile-friendly structure. Avoid jargon that does not help the decision. Every factual claim needs compatible support. Distinguish data, opinions, reports, and hypotheses. Never invent outcomes, experiments, testimonials, urgency, or scarcity.

Answer actual purchase barriers—price, quality, use, compatibility, delivery, support, privacy, trust—near the point where they arise. Explain price, terms, bonuses, deadlines, guarantees, and the next step. A bonus or scarcity does not make an offer good by itself; limited time or stock must be real. A CTA should say what the action does and must not promise a destination different from the actual flow.

For regulated claims, check evidence and permission separately. See brazilian-compliance.md and claims-matrix.md for Brazil-specific and claim-level review.

## 5. Measurement

Define the desired behavior before selecting the metric: qualified click, lead, add to cart, purchase, margin, or retention. Record the observed barrier, a testable hypothesis, primary metric, guardrails, and implementation requirements. Use controlled comparison when volume and instrumentation support it. Before/after comparisons are vulnerable to seasonality, channel, and audience mix. Record the result and its limits; no test validates a formula universally.

For sample-ratio mismatch (SRM), compare observed and expected counts with an appropriate statistical test and a preselected criterion. A raw percentage gap alone does not establish a problem; a statistical alert signals a possible data-quality issue but does not diagnose the cause. Investigate before reading treatment effects. See optimization-and-testing.md.

## 6. Historical profiles and attribution

The project includes operational profiles of 17 copywriters and three framework authors. Use profiles as editorial lenses, not as claims that one person alone created a campaign or that a method has proven universal effectiveness. Campaign work involves teams. Label author-reported performance as self-reported. Specific historical dates and popular quotations may be disputed; consult sources.md before citing them.

## 7. Practical decision sequence

For an open-ended commercial copy task, ask internally:

1. Who needs to act, and in what context?
2. What do they already know about the problem, category, product, and offer?
3. What observed barrier prevents progress?
4. Which relevant benefit and evidence can be stated?
5. What structure makes the argument easiest to follow?
6. What next step is clear, appropriate, and transparent?
7. What signal could show whether the message helped?

Research and offer define what to say; structure helps organize it; measurement helps assess what happened. Follow the requested deliverable and keep strategy notes out unless useful.
